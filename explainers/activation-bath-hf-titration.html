Why a two-endpoint titration cannot measure HF in an activation bath

An activation bath is an acid mix that strips oxide off steel just before nickel plating. The control software sets its HF level from a titration with two endpoints. This page shows what that method can and cannot see, how a counting error in the add calculation (a stoichiometry error) compounds it, and what the steel pays for the result.

Example bath. Sulfuric acid 60 oz/gal (4.58 M) plus HF 4.2 oz/gal (1.57 M), with the fluoride supplied as ammonium bifluoride (NH4HF2).

The method. A 2 mL sample is diluted to 50 mL and titrated with 1.0 N sodium hydroxide while a glass pH electrode follows the pH. Sulfuric acid is the mL used to reach pH 4.0, times 3.27 oz/gal per mL. Fluoride is the mL used from pH 4.0 to pH 7.0, times 1.34 oz/gal per mL.

4

The add calculation counts one fluoride where there are two

Ammonium bifluoride (NH4HF2) is the fluoride source. Each unit carries two fluorides. A control setup that assumes one fluoride treats the compound as 33.3% fluoride when it is 66.6%.

TakeawayEvery add is about twice what the bath needs.

5

The control loop: how the two errors hide each other

Watch three add cycles. The bath uses up HF at a steady rate. When the record falls below the lower control point, the control software orders an add sized for a 0.4 oz/gal rise. The bath gains 0.84 oz/gal, and the method reports only the fraction you set below.

How the simulation works. The bath loses HF at a steady rate, one 0.84 oz/gal drop per cycle. An add is ordered the moment the record reaches the lower control point, which was set when the bath was made up at 4.2 oz/gal. The record is the chosen fraction of the bath HF. The example method in panels 1 and 2 reports 16%.

TakeawayOn paper the errors cancel, but in the bath they stack.

What over- and under-activation do to steel before nickel plating

Activation strips oxide so nickel can bond to bare steel. Too much HF and too little HF both leave a damaged surface. The drawings are schematic cross sections, not to scale.

Adhesion can pass on all three; the pore is the failure.

TakeawayToo much and too little activation both leave a path to the steel, and a path is all salt spray needs.

7

The four things to remember

  1. 1
    The add is 2x.

    7.5 lb of NH4HF2 is ordered where 3.56 lb is needed (2.1 times), so the bath gains 0.84 oz/gal instead of 0.4 oz/gal.

  2. 2
    The method sees about 10% of the HF.

    13% in the example (0.41 mL of the 3.14 mL of HF falls in the window), and 8 to 14% across plausible pKa values.

  3. 3
    The record stays in band.

    An add that is 2.1 times too big, read by a method that reports 50%, shows up as the 0.4 oz/gal rise the software expects.

  4. 4
    The steel takes the difference.

    Over-activated or under-activated steel leaves pores under the nickel, and a pore is where red rust starts in salt spray.

TakeawayA calm record does not mean a calm bath.
