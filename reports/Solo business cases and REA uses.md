# Undercut the labor, not the data

A solo developer's best first business in late 2026 is a fixed-fee **EU Cyber Resilience Act "firmware evidence pack"**. It bundles an SBOM, vulnerability triage with reachability notes, hardening findings and a vulnerability-handling runbook. It runs on free, Apache-licensed Ghidra (above all its BSim function-matching database), with REA as the agent glue, and it is sold to small and medium connected-product makers before the CRA applies in full on **11 December 2027**. No other candidate in this research passes all six tests at once. Buyers are legally forced to act, the incumbents are enterprise-only and price by quote, tool licences cost nothing, legal risk is the lowest in the set (the customer owns the firmware), there is a fixed deadline, and the work builds a reusable asset over time. On the "undercut an expensive incumbent" track, the PimEyes pattern of reselling commodity data at subscription prices has already been competed away in lookups. VIN, IMEI and image checks now sell for $0–10, and Carfax keeps growing because its data is proprietary. The margin that remains sits in **labor-priced deliverables that LLMs now automate**: accessibility audits and conformance reports, review management at $299–449 per location per month, and 25–50% contingency fees on property-tax appeals. Those are the three best plays outside REA, with accessibility first. The risks are real. Nobody has published what buyers will pay for CRA services. Free tools already produce raw SBOMs. Micro and small firms are exempt from fines for missing the 24-hour early warning. REA is a three-month-old project with one maintainer, and its 154k GitHub stars say nothing about adoption. Every AI-derived conclusion needs human sign-off. If you cannot read embedded firmware and won't spend a month learning, start with the accessibility play instead.

## Cheap lookups are already arbitraged; labor-priced work is not

PimEyes charges a subscription for what is mostly an index plus embeddings. That model has been copied everywhere the input is data, and in those markets the price gap is already gone. In vehicle history, Carfax charges about $44.99 per report ([Collegian](https://collegian.com/sponsored/2026/05/3-best-carfax-alternatives-in-2026/)). VinAudit, an approved NMVTIS provider, sells reports for $9.99 or $20 a month unlimited and says wholesale NMVTIS data costs **$1 per report, falling to $0.25 at volume** ([VinAudit](https://www.vinaudit.com/?p=22297)). EpicVIN sells packs at $5.40 per report ([EpicVIN](https://epicvin.com/price)). Even so, **Carfax segment revenue rose 8% to $312M in Q2 2026** ([BusinessWire](https://secure.businesswire.com/news/home/20260806846937/en/Mobility-Global-Reports-Second-Quarter-2026-Financial-Results)). Cheap public data does not beat proprietary service and accident records, and the cheap tier is already crowded. IMEI checks show the same pattern: about €0.085 wholesale against $5–15 retail, with many SEO resellers ([M360](https://m360soft.com/help/others)). So do résumé scanners, where Jobscan's roughly $49.95 a month faces free competitors ([Interview Guys](https://blog.theinterviewguys.com/?p=10428)).

Exact-match reverse image search for catfish checks looks like the closest clean analog to PimEyes. Social Catfish charges about $28.97 a month ([Futurepedia](https://www.futurepedia.io/tool/social-catfish)), while Google's Web Detection API costs **$3.50 per 1,000 images** ([Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/vision/pricing?hl=bg)). It still fails the screen. Illinois BIPA plaintiffs have sued over technology "involving a face in any way," including passport-photo software ([Duane Morris](https://blogs.duanemorris.com/classactiondefense/2024/06/20/illinois-federal-court-rejects-class-action-because-an-ai-powered-porn-filter-does-not-violate-the-bipa/)). Google Lens is free, and the core use case, finding out who a stranger really is, sits next to people search.

The surviving margin is in categories where the price tracks the customer's outcome or a professional's hours rather than the data. Property-tax firms take **25–50% of savings** ([Ownwell](https://www.ownwell.com/blog/ownwell-lower-percentage-pricing-better)). Rocket Money takes 35–60% of first-year bill savings ([Rocket Money](https://help.rocketmoney.com/en/articles/9744474-bill-negotiation-charge)). Medical-bill advocates take 10–35%, some with $800 minimums ([KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/health-industry/advocate-sidebar/); [CareRoute](https://www.careroute.ai/bill-defense/compare-medical-bill-negotiation-services)). In B2B, manual accessibility audits cost **$100–250 per page** ([Accessible.org](https://accessible.org/pricing/)). Compute is effectively free by comparison. At list prices of $0.10/$0.50 per million tokens for Claude Haiku 5.5 and $2/$10 for Sonnet 5.5 ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)), a ten-page generated report costs roughly half a cent on Haiku and ten cents on Sonnet. A solo product's real costs are customer acquisition, support, and whatever human accountability the deliverable has to carry.

The legal screen removes several targets that look attractive. Ticket contesting and any "robot lawyer" framing invite unauthorized-practice claims. The FTC's final order against DoNotPay imposed **$193,000** and banned unsubstantiated lawyer-equivalence claims ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/87474)). Flight compensation looks lucrative at AirHelp's 35% plus a 15% legal fee ([AirHelp](https://airhelp.com/en/price-list/)). But airlines such as Ryanair pay direct claims in full ([Ryanair](https://help.ryanair.com/hc/en-ie/articles/12890736320529-Beware-of-claims-chasers)), and the claims firms' real moat is litigation. USCIS certified translation is legally clean, since it needs a translator's certification but no license or notarization ([ImmiHelp](https://www.immihelp.com/what-is-a-certified-translation-for-uscis)). It is still a staffing business: the $25–40 page price is mostly paying for a competent human certifier, not for machine translation at about $25 per million characters ([SimpleLocalize](https://simplelocalize.io/blog/posts/ai-machine-translation-cost-comparison/)). In audit-adjacent markets, low price is now a liability. The March 2026 Delve allegations of near-identical SOC 2 reports damaged trust in cheap compliance ([IANS Research](https://www.iansresearch.com/resources/all-blogs/post/security-blog/2026/04/19/delve-allegations-expose-weak-points-in-modern-compliance)), so SOC 2 readiness is not a pure price play.

## Accessibility, review management and tax appeals lead the undercut field

Three plays survive all six criteria well enough to launch solo. They are ranked by how well forced demand, recurring revenue and a reachable channel line up.

| Play | Incumbent price | Delivery cost | Demand evidence | Legal risk | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Accessibility "fix, don't overlay" monitor + VPAT/ACR drafts | Audits $5k–25k; $100–250/page; ACR $350–950 plus audit; overlays $49–249/mo | Cents per scan and LLM fix | 3,117 federal web suits in 2025 (+27%) | Moderate: never claim compliance (FTC accessiBe order) | **Best undercut play** |
| Flat-fee review and Google Business Profile automation | Birdeye $299–449/location/mo, annual lock-in; Podium $399–599 | About $0.001 per drafted reply | FTC Consumer Review Rule warning letters, Dec 2025 | Low if requests are never gated | Strong; agency channel |
| Property-tax appeal evidence packet | 25–50% contingency; AppealDesk $49 flat | Comps data plus LLM (data price unverified) | Ownwell 500k+ customers, +180% in 2025 | Low if the owner files | Proven demand, already being undercut |
| Cookie consent with GPC verification | Cookiebot €7–90/domain/mo, reportedly doubled Aug 2025 | CDN-served script | California fines up to $2.75M (Disney) | Moderate: failed blocking leads to fines | Crowded; runner-up |
| Medical-bill audit and dispute kit | 10–35% contingency, $800 minimums | Under $0.10 per bill | VC-funded entrants | FTC Health Breach Notification Rule | Viable, with sensitive data |

**Accessibility** is the clearest case of a must-buy category where incumbents overcharge. Seyfarth counted **3,117 federal website-accessibility suits in 2025, up 27%** ([Seyfarth](https://www.adatitleiii.com/2026/03/federal-court-website-accessibility-lawsuit-filings-bounce-back-in-2025/)). UsableNet counted 4,928 once state courts are included ([UsableNet](https://blog.usablenet.com/ada-web-lawsuit-trends-2026)). A secondary report says only 36% of sued companies had more than $25M in revenue ([Newswire](https://www.newswire.com/news/digital-accessibility-lawsuits-surge-in-2025-22618502)). The incumbents fall into two camps, and both are exposed. Human audits run **$5,000–25,000** for mid-market sites ([WebAbility](https://www.webability.io/blog/wcag-audit-cost)). Overlays have been discredited: the FTC made accessiBe pay **$1M** and barred it from claiming automated WCAG compliance ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/88115)), and in August 2026, 134 of 432 sued defendants were already running a widget ([TestParty citing UsableNet](https://testparty.ai/blog/accessibility-widget-lawsuit-data)). The gap in the middle is a Shopify/WordPress or GitHub app that scans on a schedule, proposes source-level fixes as pull requests, and drafts an accessibility statement and a VPAT 2.5 report. Buyers are now asking for that document: the University of Vermont's June 2026 rubric rejects VPAT templates older than 2.5 and asks for an update within 24 months ([UVM](https://www.uvm.edu/d10-files/documents/2026-06/Padlet-ACR-Evaluation-June-2026-ACH.pdf)). Accessible.org charges $350–950 per VPAT on top of an audit ([Accessible.org](https://accessible.org/cost-vpat/)). Price points of $29–99 a month for stores and a few hundred dollars per report refresh undercut both camps. There are two constraints. Automated scanning catches only about 30–40% of WCAG issues ([WebAbility](https://www.webability.io/blog/wcag-audit-cost)), and DOJ cited "limits of generative artificial intelligence for remediation" when it pushed Title II deadlines to **April 2027 and 2028** ([CFS Law Monitor](https://www.consumerfinancialserviceslawmonitor.com/2026/04/doj-extends-title-ii-ada-web-accessibility-rule-compliance-deadlines-for-state-and-local-governments/)). The product must be sold as monitoring plus fixes plus documentation, never as "compliance." The space is also crowded. An unverified scan of X found that the loudest complaints about ADA and SOC 2 costs came from vendors pitching rival tools, not from buyers (for example, [@polsia](https://x.com/polsia/status/2084426759030948156)).

**Review management** has the widest price-to-cost ratio in the research. Birdeye charges $299–449 per location per month, billed annually, and has a 1.7/5 Trustpilot score with complaints about cancellations not being honored ([CostBench](https://www.costbench.com/compare/birdeye-vs-podium/)). A drafted reply costs about a tenth of a cent on GPT-5.4-mini ([Morph](https://morphllm.com/openai-api-pricing)). The FTC's Consumer Review Rule gives a compliance angle. In December 2025 the FTC sent warning letters citing penalties of up to **$53,088 per violation** for review gating and suppression ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/blogs/consumer-products-and-retail-navigator/2026/01/ftc-warning-letters-over-consumer-review-rule)). That lets a $19–49 month-to-month product sell itself as "we never gate." Local-marketing agencies can white-label it, which spares one person from selling to small businesses directly. The weaknesses are that nothing forces the purchase and that Google Business Profile API approval was not verified.

**Property-tax appeals** have the best-proven consumer demand. Ownwell has 500,000+ customers, grew 180% in 2025 and raised a $50M Series B in February 2026 ([Inman](https://www.inman.com/2026/02/25/ownwell-raises-50m-to-grow-its-property-tax-appeal-fintech/)). It still charges 25–35%. AppealDesk already sells a **$49 flat-fee** comps packet that the owner files ([AppealDesk](https://www.appealdesk.com/compare/ownwell-alternative)), which validates the price point and also takes the first-mover spot. Ownwell's own National Appeals Packet is described as free by one outlet ([Commercial Observer](https://commercialobserver.com/?p=561620)). If that holds, Ownwell is giving away the very product a solo builder would sell. Selling evidence is clean. Representing taxpayers for pay in Texas requires TDLR registration ([TDLR](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/media/pdf/PTC%20at%20a%20Glance.pdf)). The work is seasonal, depends on SEO county by county, and runs on comps data whose price this research did not establish. Cookie consent with tracker-blocking verification is a good fourth choice. Cookiebot reportedly doubled its base price in August 2025 ([ConsentStack](https://www.consentstack.io/blog/cookiebot-pricing)), and California fines have punished banners that did not actually block trackers or honor Global Privacy Control, including Honda, Healthline and Disney ([Privisy](https://www.getprivisy.com/enforcement)). But a failed blocker exposes the customer to fines, and the market is crowded.

## Ghidra is the durable asset; REA is replaceable glue

REA is an MIT-licensed MCP server and CLI. It connects 21 coding agents, including Claude Code, Codex and Cursor, to local analysis engines, and returns every result as an evidence record with its stated limitations ([REA README](https://github.com/morluto/rea/blob/main/README.md)). It coordinates other engines rather than being a new one, and it is very young. The repo was created on 14 April 2026, the first npm release came on 12 July, and it has shipped **35 releases and six major versions in three months** ([CHANGELOG](https://github.com/morluto/rea/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)), all published from one maintainer's npm account ([npm registry](https://registry.npmjs.org/rea-agents)). Its popularity figures should not be taken at face value. The GitHub API showed **154,401 stars and 35,531 forks on 12 October**, against about 12,962 stars four days earlier ([AICoder](https://aicoder.com/news/news-20261008-rea-reverse-engineer-anything-mcp-agents)). npm downloads are reportedly only about 1,300 a week ([ringozzt/rea-research](https://github.com/ringozzt/rea-research)). This research found no documented case of anyone earning money with REA. Use it as convenient glue, pin a version, and design the business so REA could be removed without losing the product.

Licensing decides which stack can be sold. Only the open-source path is clean for a paid service:

| Engine | Price | Fit for a paid service |
|---|---|---|
| Ghidra 12.1.4 | Free, Apache 2.0 | Yes |
| JADX, Apktool, Binwalk/Unblob | Free, Apache/MIT | Yes |
| IDA Pro | Commercial; REA states its bridge grants no hosting or service rights | Only with a negotiated licence |
| JEB Pro | $2,000 per user per year; floating seat $4,000 | Licence limits on disclosing decompiled output to third parties conflict with client deliverables |
| Hopper | $99 personal, $129 per computer | REA's Linux path automates the demo mode, which is not a basis for a commercial service |

Sources: [THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES](https://github.com/morluto/rea/blob/main/THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES.md), [REA IDA provider docs](https://github.com/morluto/rea/blob/main/docs/ida-provider.md), [JEB licences](https://pnfsoftware.com/jeb/buy), [Hopper store](https://hopperapp.com/webstore). The full Hex-Rays and Hopper licence texts were not read, so an operator should confirm them, or simply stay on Ghidra.

Ghidra is the part worth building on. It is free, maintained by the NSA with roughly two feature releases a year, and REA is tested against its current release. Its two most commercially useful features are not wrapped by REA. **BSim** finds functions that resemble known code. **Version Tracking** (and the open-source ghidriff tool built on Ghidra, [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/ghidriff)) compares two builds of a program. That gap is the opening. Free SBOM tools identify components mostly from file names and version strings, and cve-bin-tool's own documentation warns its results "may be incomplete or inaccurate" ([cve-bin-tool docs](https://cve-bin-tool.readthedocs.io/en/latest/how_to_guides/sbom.html)). A private BSim reference library of common embedded components (OpenSSL, zlib, BusyBox, mbedTLS, lwIP, FreeRTOS), built once and reused on every customer, finds components those tools miss. It also grows more valuable with every engagement, which a solo service otherwise lacks.

The market already has funded players. Free single-engine bridges are well adopted: ida-pro-mcp has about 12.7k stars and GhidraMCP about 11.1k. Commercial vendors are moving in. Binary Ninja's Sidekick costs $30–300 a month ([Sidekick pricing](https://sidekick.binary.ninja/pricing/)). ONEKEY launched an AI firmware-analysis agent on 1 September 2026, timed to the CRA ([Security Today](https://securitytoday.com/articles/2026/09/02/onekey-launches-ai-agent-for-automated-firmware-security-analysis.aspx)). RevEng.AI raised a $15M Series A in May 2026 ([SecurityWeek](https://www.securityweek.com/reveng-ai-raises-15-million-to-hunt-for-flaws-and-backdoors-in-software-binaries/amp/)). These vendors sell quote-only platforms to large manufacturers. The opening is the small manufacturer that cannot buy or run an enterprise platform.

Operating safely is part of the cost. REA states plainly that it "is not a sandbox" ([SECURITY.md](https://github.com/morluto/rea/blob/main/SECURITY.md)). 2026 research also shows that files under analysis can carry text designed to manipulate the AI agent reading them ([arXiv 2605.30677](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.30677)). Customer files must be processed on disposable, isolated machines that hold no credentials, and every AI-derived finding needs human review before it reaches a customer.

## The CRA firmware evidence pack is the best REA business

The Cyber Resilience Act creates a purchase that small manufacturers cannot avoid. Article 14 reporting has applied since **11 September 2026**, to products already on the market. It requires an early warning within 24 hours of an actively exploited vulnerability or severe incident, a notification within 72 hours, and a final report within 14 days or one month ([DIGITAL SME Alliance](https://www.digitalsme.eu/events/charting-the-route-to-cyber-resilience-act-compliance-for-smes/); [CRA reporting guide](https://www.cyberresilienceact.eu/reporting.html)). ENISA accepts reports through a web form and has said it will not offer an API at this stage. The full obligations, including a machine-readable SBOM and CE marking, apply from **11 December 2027**, with top-tier fines of €15M or 2.5% of worldwide turnover ([cvdportal](https://cvdportal.com/cra/articles)). One caveat matters for pricing: Article 64(10) exempts micro and small enterprises from fines for missing the 24-hour early-warning deadlines ([StreamLex Art. 64](https://streamlex.eu/articles/cra-en-art-64/)). For the smallest firms, the real deadline is December 2027: without CE marking they cannot sell their product in the EU.

Readiness is low. An ENISA survey found that about 35% of responding organisations (67 of 194) use SBOMs ([summary](https://www.cyberresilienceact.eu/hu/news/enisa-sme-cra-survey-high-awareness-low-readiness.html)). The Linux Foundation found in June 2026 that 32% of manufacturers produce SBOMs for all products, and only 41% expect full compliance by December 2027 ([Linux Foundation](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/blog/the-cra-readiness-reality-what-changed-and-what-didnt-between-2025-and-2026)). The standards will not rescue them soon. FprEN 40000-1-1 and -1-2 passed a formal vote, with ratification due 2 November 2026, but are reportedly not expected to be cited in the Official Journal ([craevidence tracker](https://craevidence.com/cra-compliance/harmonised-standards-status); unverified against primary sources). Manufacturers therefore cannot rely on a standard to presume conformity. They have to show evidence against Annex I directly, and that evidence is exactly what this service would sell.

**What to sell is the deliverable, not the tool.** Free tools (EMBA, Syft, cve-bin-tool) and Binarly's free tier ([FitGap](https://us.fitgap.com/products/028846/binarly)) already turn out raw SBOMs, so "an SBOM" alone is worth nothing. The paid product has two parts:

- **Onboarding pack, fixed fee per product.** It contains an SBOM that includes components identified by the BSim reference library, and vulnerability triage with "affected / not affected" (VEX) statements and the evidence for each. It also covers hardening findings mapped to Annex I, and a vulnerability-handling and Article 14 reporting runbook with templates.
- **Monitoring subscription.** It re-checks the SBOM against new vulnerability and known-exploited lists, drafts the 24-hour early-warning text when something affects the product, and verifies what changed in each new firmware release.

No public price data for CRA services exists; every incumbent quotes privately. Unverified leads from an external model put consultant engagements at €2k–10k and one self-serve tool at €399 a month. A testable starting hypothesis is €1.5k–5k per product pack plus €150–500 a month for monitoring. That is an estimate to validate in sales conversations, not a researched figure.

Some things stay with humans no matter how good the automation gets. These include the risk assessment, the technical file, the support-period decision, the coordinated-disclosure policy, and the decision to file an ENISA report. "Important class II" and "critical" products also need a notified body. The service should present itself as evidence and documentation, never as certification. It should carry professional-liability (E&O) insurance. It should sell through channels that already have the buyer's trust: SME associations, embedded and IoT communities, and CRA consultancies that need a technical subcontractor.

**Other REA plays, in order:**

1. **FDA Section 524B evidence, sold white-label to medical-device consultancies.** The FDA has refused cyber-device submissions lacking an SBOM and vulnerability plans since 1 October 2023 ([Keyfactor](https://www.keyfactor.com/compliance-hub/fda-guidance/)). Illumina's $9.8M False Claims Act settlement shows the liability ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/illumina-inc-pay-98m-resolve-false-claims-act-allegations-arising-cybersecurity)). It reuses the CRA pipeline. Sell to the consultancies rather than to device makers. An external model's claim that a $999 SBOM "undercuts $65k–150k packages by 98%" does not hold: those packages cover the whole cybersecurity submission, and a regulated filing will not rest on a solo vendor's unsupervised report.
2. **Checking that a mobile app's privacy disclosures match what its SDKs actually do.** The amended COPPA Rule has been enforceable since 22 April 2026 and requires separate parental consent for third-party advertising disclosures ([Bass Berry](https://bassberry.com/news/amendments-to-the-coppa-rule-now-in-effect/)). The FTC's Apitor case held a developer responsible for an embedded SDK's data collection ([Foley Hoag](https://foleyhoag.com/news-and-insights/blogs/security-privacy-and-the-law/2025/september/ftc-to-app-developers-your-vendors-coppa-missteps-are-your-own/)). California settled with Jam City for $1.4M ([CA AG](https://www.oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-secures-14-million-settlement-mobile-app-gaming-company)). The best channel is privacy law firms and app agencies. Analyse only the client's own apps.
3. **Data-export converters for closed industry software.** Each converter reads a customer's own locally held data so they can migrate. Dental conversions already sell for about $1,200 per database ([Open Dental](https://www.OpenDental.com/site/conversions.html)) or $800–1,400 ([Benco](https://www.benco.com/?p=23112)), and one decoder can be resold many times. The legal edges are real. US courts enforce licence clauses that ban reverse engineering (*Bowers v. Baystate*, *Davidson v. Jung*). In the EU, the *Top System* ruling and the Software Directive protect error correction and interoperability but not building similar programs. Incumbents fight over data access: a $100M dealer class settlement covered the CDK and Reynolds systems ([Dayton Daily News](https://www.daytondailynews.com/business/court-weigh-reynolds-and-reynolds-settlement/dJnhyCtFlALgUwFsDA3HPJ)). The EU Data Act helps going forward. It requires connected products placed on the market after 12 September 2026 to provide user data access by design, but bars using that data to build competing products ([IAM Media](https://www.iam-media.com/hub/trade-secrets-hub/2025/article/connected-products-and-data-sharing-how-the-eu-data-act-reshaping-trade-secret-protection-in-europe)).
4. **Lost-source recovery and rebuilds of legacy business software.** Agencies quote €40k–110k to rebuild a small VB6 or Access application. A solo clean-room rebuild at $15k–60k is plausible (an estimate from the notes). It is project work rather than a product, and REA's docs do not cover VB6, Delphi or 16-bit Windows.

**Not recommended:**

- **Competitor feature teardowns,** which are REA's own headline pitch: licence clauses, EU limits on reusing decompiled information, and the Data Act's competing-product bar.
- **Bug bounties as a business:** earnings are concentrated, and AI agents now compete.
- **Smart-contract due diligence:** crowded.
- **The US Cyber Trust Mark:** voluntary and stalled.
- **Federal SBOM work:** weakened after OMB M-26-05 rescinded the secure-software attestation ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/02/omb-rescinds-biden-era-software-security-memoranda)).
- **Scanning other people's published apps for leaked secrets:** that analyses third-party software without authorisation.

## Start with the CRA pack if you can read firmware; otherwise accessibility

The choice turns on one skill. If you can read embedded firmware, or will spend a month learning, start the CRA evidence pack. It has the strongest forcing function, the least price competition at the small-company end, and an asset (the BSim reference library) that compounds. If not, start the accessibility play. It needs only web skills, sells through app stores, and has lawsuit-driven demand.

**A 90-day plan for the CRA pack:**

| Weeks | Work | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | Build the BSim reference library for 10–15 common embedded components, and run the pipeline end to end on open-source firmware you are free to analyse (e.g. OpenWrt builds) | A sample evidence pack to use as the sales demo |
| 3–4 | Write templates for the SBOM/VEX report, hardening findings, Article 14 runbook and early-warning draft; get an E&O quote; write terms that frame the work as evidence, not certification | Sellable package and contract |
| 5–8 | Run 3–5 discounted pilots with small manufacturers, analysing only firmware they own under written authorisation, on isolated machines, with every finding reviewed by you | Paid pilots and real price feedback |
| 9–12 | Convert pilots to monitoring subscriptions; approach 2–3 CRA consultancies for white-label work; publish content on the Article 14 runbook | First recurring revenue |

Set a kill criterion before starting. If fewer than three of the first 30 serious conversations lead to a paid pilot, switch to accessibility, where the tooling is cheaper to build and buyers can self-serve.

**The accessibility play in brief:** a Shopify, WordPress or GitHub app that scans on a schedule, proposes source-level fixes as pull requests, and drafts an accessibility statement and a VPAT 2.5 report. Price it at $29–99 a month per store plus a fee per report refresh. Never claim compliance or lawsuit protection.

## What could change this ranking

- **CRA demand is real, but its price is unknown.** No buyer has published what it pays, and enterprise vendors such as ONEKEY could move down-market.
- **The fine exemption softens urgency for the smallest firms.** Micro and small manufacturers are not fined for missing the 24-hour warning, so the sale should lead with December 2027 CE marking.
- **REA is unproven.** It is three months old, has one maintainer and has inflated stars. The business should depend on Ghidra and on your own pipeline, not on REA.
- **Several legal and regulatory facts rest on secondary sources.** The research environment blocked EUR-Lex, the Copyright Office, CEN-CENELEC and the FDA. These include the CRA standards status, whether ENISA's reporting platform is live, and the exact statutory texts. Have counsel check them before signing customers.
- **External model inputs were partly wrong.** The user supplied answers from other models (Grok and one other). They agreed on the CRA and accessibility but made errors. They compared a $999 SBOM with full FDA submission packages. Their Google Trends percentages were unsourced. They priced LLM work on a retired model. Their trucking Clearinghouse idea described the penalties incorrectly. Their DMARC price gap ignored free monitoring tools (to verify). Those ideas are not ranked here. The FMCSA Clearinghouse automation idea is the one worth a separate verification pass.
